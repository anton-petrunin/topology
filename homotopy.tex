\chapter{Homotopy}

\section{Homotopy}\label{sec:homotopy}

Two continuous maps $f,g\:\c{X}\to \c{Y}$ are called \index{homotopic}\emph{homotopic} (briefly, $f\sim g$) if $f$ can be continuously deformed into $g$.
Formally, this means that there exists a continuous map
\[
H\:\c{X}\times[0,1]\to \c{Y}
\]
such that $H(x,0)=f(x)$ and $H(x,1)=g(x)$ for all $x\in \c{X}$.
The map $H\:\c{X}\times[0,1]\to \c{Y}$ is called \index{homotopy}\emph{homotopy} from $f$ to $g$.

It is often convenient to think of the homotopy $H$ as a one-parameter family of maps $h_t\:\c{X}\to \c{Y}$ defined by $h_t(x)=H(x,t)$.

We say that a map is \index{null-homotopic}\emph{null-homotopic} if it is homotopic to a constant map.

\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:comp-hom}
Let $f_0, f_1\:\c{X}\to\c{Y}$ and $g_0,g_1\:\c{Y}\to \c{Z}$ be
four maps between topological spaces.
Assume that $f_0\sim f_1$ and $g_0\sim g_1$.
Show that $g_0\circ f_0\sim g_1\circ f_1$.
\end{thm}

\section{Linear homotopy}

Suppose $\c{C}$ is a convex subset of $\RR^n$ with induced topology.
Given two continuous maps $f,g\:\c{X}\to \c{C}$, one can consider \emph{linear homotopy} from $f$ to $g$ defined by
\[H(x,t)=(1-t)\cdot f(x)+t\cdot g(x).\]
It follows that any two continuous maps $\c{X}\to\c{C}$ are homotopic;
in particular, any continuous map $\c{X}\to\c{C}$ is null-homotopic.

Recall that
\[\SS^2\df\set{(x,y,z)\in\RR^3}{x^2+y^2+z^2=1}.\]

\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:homot-sphere}
Suppose that for two continuous maps $f_0, f_1\:\c{X}\z\to\SS^2$, we have that $f_0(x)\z+f_1(x)\ne 0$ for any $x\in\c{X}$.
Show that $f_0\sim f_1$.
\end{thm}




\section{Relative homotopy}\label{sec:rel-homotopy}

Let $H\:\c{X}\times[0,1]\to \c{Y}$ be a homotopy, and let $A\subset \c{X}$.
Suppose that for any $a\in A$ the point $H(a,t)$ does not depend on $t$.
Then we say that $H$ is a homotopy \index{relative homotopy}\emph{relative to $A$}.
Two maps $f,g\: \c{X}\to \c{Y}$ are called \index{homotopic relative to $A$}\emph{homotopic relative to $A$}, briefly, $f\sim g\rel A$ if there is a homotopy relative to $A$ from $f$ to $g$.

Note also that if $f\sim g\rel A$, then $f$ agrees with $g$ on $A$;
that is, $f(a)=g(a)$ for any $a\in A$.

The properties of homotopies that we are about to describe admit straightforward generalizations to its relative version.

\section{Operations on homotopies}\label{sec:homot-operations}

The following operations on homotopies are analogous to the operations on paths discussed in \ref{sec:Operations on paths}.
In fact, a path is a special case of homotopy --- it is a homotopy with one-point source space.
\begin{itemize}
\item the \index{constant homotopy}\emph{constant homotopy} $H(x,t)=f(x)$ joins $f$ with itself;
\item if $H$ is a homotopy from $f$ to $g$, then the
\index{time-reversed}\emph{time-reversed} homotopy defined by
\[
\bar H(x,t)=H(x,1-t)
\]
is a homotopy from $g$ to $f$;
\item Consider two homotopies $H$ and $G$ from $f$ to $g$ and from $g$ to $h$, respectively.
Then their \index{concatenation of homotopies}\emph{concatenation} $F=H*G$ defined by
\[
F(x,t)=
\begin{cases}
H(x,2\cdot t),& t\le \tfrac12,\\
G(x,2\cdot t-1),& t\ge \tfrac12,
\end{cases}
\]
is a homotopy from $f$ to $h$.
(By \ref{ex:u-continuous:closed}, $F$ is continuous.)
\end{itemize}

Recall that
\[\SS^1\df\set{(x,y)\in\RR^2}{x^2+y^2=1}.\]

\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:homot-S1}
Let $f\:\SS^1\z\to\SS^1$, be a continuous map such that
$f(x)\ne x$ for any $x$.
Show that $f$ is homotopic to the identity map.
\end{thm}

\section{Homotopy classes}

The previous section implies that homotopy defines an equivalence relation $\sim$ on the set of continuous maps $\c{X}\to\c{Y}$; in other words, the following properties hold for any continuous maps $f,g,h\: \c{X}\to\c{Y}$:
\begin{itemize}
 \item $f\sim f$;
 \item if $f\sim g$, then $g\sim f$;
 \item if $f\sim g$, and $g\sim h$, then $f\sim h$.
\end{itemize}

The equivalence classes for $\sim$ are called \index{homotopy class}\emph{homotopy classes};
the equivalence class of a map $f\:\c{X}\to\c{Y}$ will be denoted by $[f]$.
Given two spaces $\c{X}$ and $\c{Y}$, the set of all homotopy classes will be denoted by
$\pi(\c{X},\c{Y})$.

Often the set $\pi(\c{X},\c{Y})$ admits a direct description; this is precisely its advantage over the vast set of all continuous maps $\c{X}\to \c{Y}$.
Note that any homeomorphism $\c{Y}\to \c{Y}'$ defines a bijection between homotopy classes $\pi(\c{X},\c{Y})\to \pi(\c{X},\c{Y}')$ for any given space $\c{X}$.
So, studying the homotopy classes may help to distinguish topological spaces.


This construction is meaningful even for very simple choices of source space $\c{X}$.
If $\c{X}$ is the one-point space, then $\pi(\c{X},\c{Y})$ is also denoted by $\pi_0(\c{Y})$.
Note that $\pi_0(\c{Y})$ is the set of path-connected components in $\c{Y}$ --- this was the main subject of the previous chapter.
The set $\pi(\SS^1,\c{Y})$ is closely related to the fundamental group of $\c{Y}$, which will be soon introduced.

The idea to use homotopy classes to distiguish topological spaces will guide us in the right direction.
However, the true reason to consider homotopy classes is different; it will be described in Capter \ref{chap:algebra}.

\section{Retracts}

Let $A$ be a subset in a topological space $\c{X}$.
A continuous map $r\:\c{X}\z\to A$ is called a \index{retraction}\emph{retraction} to $A$ if $r(a)=a$ for any $a\in A$.%
\footnote{The notion of retraction is closely related to projection in linear algebra; say, a continuous map $r\:\c{X}\to \c{X}$ defines a retraction to $r(\c{X})$ if and only if $r\circ r=r$.}
In this case $A$ is called a \index{retract}\emph{retract} of $\c{X}$.
If in addition, $r\:\c{X}\to A$ is homotopic to the identity map $\id_{\c{X}}$, then $A$ is called a \index{weak deformation retract}\emph{weak deformation retract} of $\c{X}$.
If $r\sim \id_{\c{X}}\rel A$, then $A$ is called \index{deformation retract}\emph{deformation retract} of $\c{X}$.

Note that a one-point subset is a retract of any space --- the retraction maps the whole space to this single point.
On the other hand, if a one-point set $\{p\}$ is a deformation retract of space $\c{X}$, then any point can be connected to $p$ by a path; in particular, $\c{X}$ has to be path-connected.

\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:retract-closed}
Show that a retract of a Hausdorff space has to be a closed subset.
\end{thm}

Given an integer $n\ge0$, denote by $J_n$ the closed line segment from the origin to the point $(1,\tfrac1{2^n})$ in $\RR^2$, and let $J_\infty$ be the closed line segment from the origin to the point $(1,0)$.

\begin{subthm}{ex:retract-strong-weak:retract}
Show that $J_0$ is a deformation retract of $F$.
\end{subthm}

\end{subthm}

\end{thm}



\section{Contractible spaces}

A topological space $\c{X}$ is called \index{contractible space}\emph{contractible} if the identity map $\id_\c{X}\:\c{X}\z\to \c{X}$ is homotopic to a constant map;
that is, there is a homotopy $h_t\:\c{X}\to\c{X}$ such that $h_0(x)=x$ and $h_1(x)=p$ for some fixed point $p\in\c{X}$ and any $t$.

Soon we will show that the circle $\mathbb{S}^1$ is an example of path-connected space that is not contractible;
in particular, there are noncontractible path-connected spaces.

Applying linear homotopy, one can see that any convex subset of the Euclidean space is contractible.
This provides a good source of contractible spaces.

\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:retract-contractible}
Show that a retract of contractible space is contractible.
\end{thm}

\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:contractible}
Let $\c{X}$ be a contractible space.

\begin{subthm}{ex:contractible:path-connected}
Show that $\c{X}$ is path-connected.

\end{subthm}

\begin{subthm}{ex:contractible:target}
Show that any two continuous maps from a topological space to $\c{X}$ are homotopic.
\end{subthm}

\begin{subthm}{ex:contractible:sourse}
Show that any two continuous maps from $\c{X}$ to a path-connected space are homotopic.
\end{subthm}

\end{thm}



\section{Homotopy type}

Two topological spaces $\c{X}$ and $\c{Y}$ have the same \index{homotopy type}\emph{homotopy type} (briefly $\c{X}\sim\c{Y}$) if there are continuous maps
$f\: \c{X}\to \c{Y}$ and $h\:\c{Y}\to \c{X}$ such that $h\circ f\sim \id_\c{X}$ and $f\circ h\sim \id_\c{Y}$.
In this case $f$ (as well as $h$) is called a \index{homotopy equivalence}\emph{homotopy equivalence} and $h$ is called \index{homotopy inverse}\emph{homotopy inverse} of $f$.

\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:homotopy-type}
Show that $\sim$ defines an equivalence relation on topological spaces.
(The corresponding equivalence classes are called \index{homotopy type}\emph{homotopy types}.)
\end{thm}


\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:homotopy-type-contractible}
Show that a topological space $\c{X}$ is contractible if and only if it has the same homotopy type with the one-point space.
\end{thm}


\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:homotopy-equivalence}
Show that a continuous map $f\:\c{X}\to\c{Y}$ is a homotopy equivalence if there are continuous maps $g,h\:\c{Y}\to\c{X}$ such that
$h\circ f\sim \id_\c{X}$ and $f\circ g\sim \id_\c{Y}$.
\end{thm}


\begin{thm}{Proposition}
If $A$ is a weak deformation retract of $\c{X}$, then $\c{X}$ and $A$ have the same homotopy type.
\end{thm}

\parit{Proof.}
Let $r\:\c{X}\to A$ be the retraction and $i\:A\hookrightarrow \c{X}$ the inclusion.
By definition, we have a homotopy $H$ from $\id_{\c{X}}$ to $i\circ r$, hence $\id_{\c{X}}\sim i\circ r$.
On the other hand, $r\circ i=\id_A$ since $r$ is a retraction.
Therefore $i$ and $r$ define a homotopy equivalence between $A$ and $\c{X}$.
\qeds

This proposition can be used together with \ref{ex:homotopy-type} to prove that given spaces have the same homotopy type; here is one example:

Show that the two closed connected sets in the picture have the same homotopy type.
(Each set is a union of several line segments and if it looks like they intersect, then they intersect.)
