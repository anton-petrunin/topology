\chapter{Fundamental group}\label{chap:pi1}

Let $\c{X}$ be a topological space with a chosen basepoint $x_0 \in \c{X}$.
Fix a point $s_0 \in \SS^1$, and  consider all continuous maps $\SS^1\to \c{X}$ that send $s_0$ to $x_0$.
Denote by $\pi_1(X,x_0)$ the set of homotopy classes$\rel{\{s_0\}}$ of such maps.

In this chapter we will equip $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)$ with multiplication and show that it makes this set into a group.
This will justify calling $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)$ the \textit{fundamental group} of $\c{X}$ with base point $x_0$.
The fundamental group is the first meaningful construction in algebraic topology, and the rest of the course will be largely devoted to it.

\section{Homotopy of paths}\label{sec:Homotopy of paths}

Recall that a path is a continuous map defined on the unit interval $[0,1]$.
The term \index{homotopy of paths}\emph{homotopy of paths} will be used as a shortcut for \textit{homotopy of paths relative to the ends};
in the notations of \ref{sec:rel-homotopy}, we have the source space $[0,1]$ with a two-point subset $A=\{0,1\}$.
In the rare case when we need to talk about general homotopy of paths (allowing the endpoints to move), we say \index{free homotopy}\emph{free homotopy of paths}.

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{35 mm}
\vskip-2mm
\centering
\includegraphics{mppics/pic-80}
\end{wrapfigure}

More formally, a homotopy of paths is defined by a continuous map $H\:[0,1]\times [0,1]\z\to \c{X}$ such that $H(0,\tau)=p$ and $H(1,\tau)=q$ for some points $p,q$ in the target space $\c{X}$.

As usual, it is convenient to think that homotopy is a one-parameter family of paths from $p$ to $q$; say $a_\tau\:[0,1]\to \c{X}$, where $a_\tau(t)\df H(t,\tau)$.
Two paths $b,c\:[0,1]\to \c{X}$ are called {}\emph{homotopic} (briefly $b\sim c$)
if there is a homotopy $a_\tau\:[0,1]\to \c{X}$ such that $b=a_0$ and $c=a_1$.
Recall that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation.
Therefore, we can talk about the equivalence class of a path $a$ that will be called its \index{homotopy class of paths}\emph{homotopy class} and denoted by~$[a]$.


\section{Technical claims}\label{sec:homotopy-claim}

\begin{thm}{Claim}\label{clm:product}
Suppose $a_0$ is a path from $p$ to $q$,
and
$b_0$ is a path from $q$ to $r$.
Suppose $a_0\sim a_1$ and $b_0\sim b_1$
then
\[a_0*b_0\sim a_1*b_1.\]

\end{thm}

\begin{wrapfigure}{l}{65 mm}
\vskip-8mm
 \centering
\includegraphics{mppics/pic-1305}
\end{wrapfigure}

\parit{Proof.}
Choose homotopies $a_\tau$ from $a_0$ to $a_1$, and $b_\tau$ from $b_0$ to $b_1$.
By \ref{ex:u-continuous:closed},
$c_\tau\df a_\tau*b_\tau$ is a homotopy from $c_0\z=a_0\z*b_0$ to $c_1=a_1*b_1$.
\qeds

\begin{thm}{Observation}\label{obs:homot-paths}
Let $s_0,s_1\:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ be two paths such that $s_0(0)=s_1(0)$ and $s_0(1)=s_1(1)$.
Then $s_0\sim s_1$.
Moreover, for any path $a$ we have $a\circ s_0\sim a\circ s_1$.
\end{thm}

\parit{Proof.}
The linear homotopy
\[s_\tau(t)=(1-\tau)\cdot s_0(t)+\tau \cdot s_1(t)\]
provides a homotopy from $s_0$ to $s_1$.
The conditions $s_0(0)=s_1(0)$ and $s_0(1)=s_1(1)$ imply that $s_\tau(0)$ and $s_\tau(1)$ do not depend on $\tau$;
that is, $s_\tau$ is a homotopy relative to the endpoints.

To prove the last statement, observe that $a\circ s_\tau$ is a homotopy of paths from $a\circ s_0$ to  $a\circ s_1$.
\qeds

\begin{thm}{Claim}\label{clm:homot-claims}
Let $a,b,c\:[0,1]\to\c{X}$ be paths such that $a(0)=p$, $a(1)=b(0)=q$ and $b(1)=c(0)=r$.
Then

\begin{subthm}{clm:neutral}
$e_p*a\sim a*e_q\sim  a$;
\end{subthm}

\begin{subthm}{clm:f-bar-f}
$a*\bar a\sim e_p$
and
$\bar a*a\sim e_q$;
\end{subthm}

\begin{subthm}{clm:assoc}
$(a*b)*c\sim a*(b*c)$.
\end{subthm}

\end{thm}

\parit{Proof.}
Each claim is proved by applying \ref{obs:homot-paths};
each time we rewrite the sides of the equation as $d\circ s_0$ and $d\circ s_1$ for appropriate path $d\:[0,1]\to\c{X}$ and a pair of paths $s_0,s_1\:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ that share the ends.

$s_0(0)\z=s_1(0)\z=s_2(0)=0$ and

\end{align*}

\section{Fundamental group}

Let $\c{X}$ be a topological space.
A path $a\:[0,1]\to\c{X}$ is called a \index{loop}\emph{loop} with base point $x_0\in\c{X}$ if $a(0)=a(1)=x_0$.
Since $\SS^1\cong[0,1]/\{0,1\}$ (see \ref{ex:[0,1]/0=1}),
any loop defines a map $\SS^1\to \c{X}$.

Note that if $a$ and $b$ are loops based at $x_0$, then their products are defined; moreover, $a*b$, $b*a$, $\bar a$, $\bar b$ are also loops based at $x_0$; see \ref{sec:Operations on paths}.


Recall that homotopy of paths and loops does not move their ends
and $[a]$ stands for the homotopy class of a loop (or path) $a$.
The multiplication of homotopy classes of loops based at $x_0$ is defined by
\[[a]\cdot [b]=[a*b];\]
that is, \textit{the product of homotopy classes of loops $a$ and $b$ is the homotopy class of the product $a*b$.}

Observe that the product is well-defined;
that is, if $[a_0]= [a_1]$ and $[b_0]=[b_1]$, then  $[a_0*b_0]=[a_1*b_1]$.
In other words, if $a_0\sim a_1$ and $b_0\sim b_1$, then $a_0*b_0\sim a_1*b_1$.
The latter is stated in Claim \ref{clm:product}.

Denote by $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)$ the set of all homotopy classes of loops with base at $x_0$.

\begin{thm}{Theorem}\label{thm:pi1}
The set $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)$ with the introduced multiplication is a group.
\end{thm}

The group $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)$ is called the \index{fundamental group}\emph{fundamental group} of $\c{X}$ with base point $x_0$.

\parit{Proof.}
Recall that $e_{x_0}$ denotes the constant loop at $x_0$ in $\c{X}$;
that is, $e_{x_0}(t)=x_0$ for any $t$.
We will show that the homotopy class $[e_{x_0}]$ is the neutral element of $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)$
and $[\bar a]=[a]^{-1}$, where $\bar a$ denotes the time-reversed path of $a$.

By \ref{clm:homot-claims},
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item $a*e_{x_0}\sim e_{x_0}*a\sim a$,
\item $a*\bar a\sim\bar a* a\sim e_{x_0}$,
\item $(a*b)*c\sim a*(b*c)$,
\end{enumerate}
for any loops $a$, $b$, and $c$ based at $x_0$.
And these equations imply the conditions in the definition of group.
\qeds

\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:group}
Let $\c{G}$ be a \index{topological group}\emph{topological group}; that is a group equipped with a topology such that the maps $\c{G}\times \c{G}\to \c{G}$ and $\c{G}\to \c{G}$ defined by $(g,h)\mapsto g\cdot h$ and $g\mapsto g^{-1}$ are continuous.
Show that the fundamental group $\pi_1(\c{G},1)$ is abelian;
in other words, $a_1*a_2\sim a_2*a_1$
for any two loops $a_1$ and $a_2$ with base point $1\in \c{G}$.
\end{thm}

\section{Simply-connected spaces}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{40 mm}
\vskip-5mm
\centering
\includegraphics{mppics/pic-1335}
\end{wrapfigure}

A nonempty topological space $\c{X}$ is called \index{simply-connected space}\emph{simply-connected} if for any pair of points $p,q\z\in\c{X}$ there is a unique homotopy class of paths from $p$ to $q$.
In other words, there is a path from $p$ to $q$ and any two such paths are homotopic.

Note that any simply-connected space is path-connected.

\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:sc-retract}
Show that a retract of a simply-connected space is simply-connected.

\end{thm}

Recall that a group is called \index{trivial group}\emph{trivial} if it contains only one element, which is necessarily the neutral element.
If the fundamental group $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)$ is trivial, it is common to write $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)=0$ despite that it makes no sense --- in general the group $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)$ is not commutative and so it would be more reasonable to write $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)\z=\{1\}$, meaning that $1$ is the only element of $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)$.

\begin{thm}{Proposition}\label{prop:sc=1hclass}
A path-connected space $\c{X}$ is simply-connected if and only if $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)=0$ for some (and therefore any) base point $x_0\in \c{X}$.
\end{thm}

Since $(a*b)*c\z\ne a*(b*c)$, one has to fully parenthesize products of paths.
However, I will omit parentheses when any parenthesization does the job (since $(a*b)*c\sim a*(b*c)$).
On a more formal basis, one can assume that the product is taken in the usual order;
that is, \index{$a*b*c*d$}
\[a*b*c*d\df((a*b)*c)*d.\]

\parit{Proof.}
Choose a base point $x_0\in \c{X}$.
If $\c{X}$ is simply-connected, then any loop based at $x_0$ is homotopic to $e_{x_0}$.
Hence the only-if part follows.

Now suppose $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)=0$.
Choose points $p,q\in \c{X}$.
Let $a$ and $b$ be paths from $p$ to $q$ and let $c$ and $d$ be paths from $x_0$ to $p$ and to $q$, respectively;
all these paths exist since $\c{X}$ is path-connected.

Since $\pi_1(\c{X},x_0)=0$, the loops $c*a*\bar d$, $e_{x_0}$, and $c*b*\bar d$ are homotopic;
that is,

{

\begin{wrapfigure}[3]{r}{40 mm}
\vskip-0mm
\centering
\includegraphics{mppics/pic-1340}
\end{wrapfigure}

\[c*a*\bar d\sim e_{x_0} \sim c*b*\bar d.\]

Applying \ref{clm:homot-claims}, we get
\begin{align*}
a&\sim \bar c* c *a*\bar d*d\sim
\\
&\sim\bar c* c *b*\bar d*d\sim
\\
&\sim b
\end{align*}
--- hence the result.
\qeds

}


\begin{thm}{Exercise}\label{ex:S1=>sc}\label{ex:contractible=>sc}
Show that a path-connected space $\c{X}$ is simply-connected if and only if every continuous map $f\:\SS^1\to \c{X}$ is null-homotopic.

Conclude that any contractible space is simply-connected.
\end{thm}


\begin{thm}{Proposition}\label{prop:van-kamp:sc}
Suppose that $V$ and $W$ are open simply-connected subsets of a topological space $\c{X}$ such that
$\c{X}=V\cup W$, and the set $V\cap W$ is path-connected.
Then $\c{X}$ is simply-connected.

\end{thm}


This statement is a special case of Van Kampen's theorem.
Roughly speaking, the general theorem allows one to recover the fundamental
group of $\c{X}=V\cup W$ from the fundamental groups of the open sets $V$, $W$, and $V\cap W$,
together with the way $V\cap W$ sits inside $V$ and $W$.
See also \ref{ex:vankam-nonconnect}.

Recall that if the product of paths is not parenthesized, then it is taken in the usual order;
that is, \index{$a*b*c*d$} $a*b*c*d\df((a*b)*c)*d$.

\parit{Proof.}
Let $a$ be a loop in $\c{X}$ based at $x_0\in V\cap W$.
Note that we can choose a sequence $0=t_0<t_1<\ldots<t_n=1$ such that
$a([t_{i-1},t_i])\z\subset V$, or $a([t_{i-1},t_i])\subset W$ and $a(t_i)\in V\cap W$ for each $i$.

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{45 mm}
\vskip-0mm
\centering
\includegraphics{mppics/pic-1350}
\end{wrapfigure}

Indeed, two open sets $a^{-1}(V)$ and $a^{-1}(W)$ cover $[0,1]$; let $\eps>0$ be a Lebesgue number of this cover.
So if $|t_i-t_{i-1}|<\eps$, then $a([t_{i-1},t_i])\subset V$ or $a([t_{i-1},t_i])\subset W$ for each $i$.
If $a(t_i)\notin V\cap W$ for some $i$, then it can be removed from the sequence keeping the property;
after several such removals we get the needed subdivision.

Reparametrizing $[t_{i-1},t_i]$ by $[0,1]$ we get paths $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ such that
\[a\sim a_1*\dots*a_n\]
and each $a_i$ lies completely in $V$ or $W$ and has ends in $V\cap W$.
Let us connect $x_0$ to every point $x_i=a_i(1)$ for $i<n$ by a path $b_i$ in $V\cap W$.
By \ref{clm:homot-claims},
\[a_1*\ldots*a_n{\sim}(a_1*\bar b_1)*(b_1*a_2*\bar b_2)*\ldots*(b_{n-1}*a_n).\]

Observe that each loop $a_1*\bar b_1$, $b_1*a_2*\bar b_2, \z\ldots, b_{n-1}*a_n$ lies entirely in $V$ or $W$.
Since $V$ and $W$ are simply-connected,
\[a_1*\bar b_1\sim e_{x_0},\quad
 b_1*a_2*\bar b_2\sim e_{x_0},\quad \ldots\ ,\quad b_{n-1}*a_n\sim e_{x_0}.
\]
Hence $a_1*\ldots*a_n\sim e_{x_0}$, and the statement follows.
\qeds

Show that (a) $\SS^2$ and (b) the union of a sphere and a disc bounded by the equator of the sphere
are simply-connected.

\end{thm}
